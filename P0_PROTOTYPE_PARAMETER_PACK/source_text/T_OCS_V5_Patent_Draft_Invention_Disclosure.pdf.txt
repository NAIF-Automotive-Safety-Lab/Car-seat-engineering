 CRITICAL LEGAL / ENGINEERING STATUS
 Core disclosure thesis: the invention is not a literal local gravity generator. It is a seat system that time-programs the
 mechanical state of the seat and its force paths so that seat translation, rotation, restraint interaction, compliance
 and rebound are coordinated during a crash event. The effective-gravity interpretation is an inertial-frame concept,
 g_eff(t)=g-a_seat(t), not creation of a new gravitational field.

 Design-around / differentiation candidates
 • A mechanical state machine in which a first state preserves ordinary seat adjustment, a second state arms an
 inertial or sensed trigger, a third state releases controlled ride-down, a fourth state controls rebound, and a final state
 secures the structure after the event.
 • Two parallel longitudinal load paths where the energy absorber path and seatback-rotation control path are
 physically distinct, so reducing rotation does not simply mean making the entire seat rigid.
 • A defined relationship between occupant trajectory envelope, seat travel, seatback angle, absorber work, and
 load-path continuity, with those quantities measured as explicit CAE/test observables.

 13. DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION
 13.1 System architecture. The T-OCS system comprises a vehicle interface/base, at least two longitudinal load paths,
 a seat pan, a seatback frame, one or more restraint interfaces, one or more energy management elements, and a
 locking/state-transition mechanism. The architecture is configured so that seat translation and seatback rotation can
 be controlled by distinct but coordinated mechanical paths.
 13.2 Time-programmed states. A representative sequence is: S0 normal operation; S1 event recognition/arming; S2
 controlled occupant capture and pelvis stabilization; S3 ride-down/energy dissipation; S4 rotation/rebound
 management; S5 secure post-event state. The sequence can be implemented mechanically, by sensing/actuation, or
 by a hybrid arrangement. The essential relationship is that the mechanical state of the seat changes as a function of
 event timing and/or detected dynamic state.
 13.3 Effective-force interpretation. In a non-inertial seat frame, a commanded or passive seat acceleration
 contributes an inertial term. A useful engineering interpretation is g_eff(t)=g-a_seat(t). The system does not create
 gravitational mass or a local gravitational field; it shapes contact and inertial force history.
 13.4 Ride-down. The seat carriage may translate along dual rails through one or more energy absorbing elements.
 The energy absorber may use hydraulic, pneumatic, viscoelastic, metallic crush, frictional, or hybrid behavior,
 provided the force-stroke relationship is characterized before system validation.
 13.5 Seatback rotation control. A dedicated rotation-limiting path may include bilateral links, dampers, hard stops,
 torsion elements or locking interfaces. The design intent is to avoid a false trade-off in which the seat is simply made
 rigid everywhere. The source FE study supports treating seatback rotation as a key design variable, but does not
 establish a universal target for this invention.
 13.6 Occupant trajectory management. The system controls the trajectories of pelvis, torso and head by coordinating
 the seat pan, seatback, restraint geometry and ride-down motion. Design observables include seat travel, seatback
 rotation, head excursion, pelvis translation/rotation and restraint load history.

 13.7 Redundant load paths. The preferred configuration uses left and right structural/energy paths so that a single
 local failure is not the sole route by which seat loads reach the vehicle body. Each path is intended to be
 independently measurable in testing.
 13.8 Rebound control. A return damper, directional hydraulic valve, friction element, or secondary lock may limit
 reverse motion after the primary ride-down stroke. This state is intentionally treated separately because secondary
 interior contact can occur during rebound.
 13.9 Fail-safe behavior. The primary occupant restraint path remains available even if the optional ride-down
 mechanism fails to arm. The design should default to a safe mechanical state when the trigger path is unavailable.

 15. FRONT-SEAT AND REAR-SEAT EMBODIMENTS
 Front seat embodiment. The same architecture can be packaged under a driver or front-passenger seat, with careful
 treatment of steering-column, pedal and dashboard clearance. The THOR-05F program demonstrates why
 lower-extremity and thorax loading cannot be reduced to a single scalar criterion; the source study reports complex
 and variable loading and uses extensive instrumentation. □cite not inserted in PDF.
 Rear seat 2+1 embodiment. Each outboard seat module can have a dedicated T-OCS carriage and absorber path,
 while the center module uses a shared or narrower architecture. Packaging must preserve center-console/egress
 volumes and provide independent belt anchorage strategy.
 Rear seat 3-seat embodiment. Three independent modules may be used where package width permits. Alternative
 architectures can share a common structural cross-member while preserving independent absorber/locking paths.
 The claim set should not rely on a particular number of seats.
 Why the rear-seat context matters. The 2025 NHTSA/SAE work explicitly studied how seatback rotation and occupant
 excursion vary across vehicles and dummies, while acknowledging that small samples and sled-vs-full-vehicle
 differences limit direct injury conclusions.

 Patent drafting principle. Describe the invention as a family of architectures capable of use in front and rear seating,
 but claim the mechanism generically enough that the inventive concept is not trapped by a single packaging
 embodiment.

 18. CLAIM STRATEGY, ALTERNATIVE EMBODIMENTS, AND
 ABSTRACT
 Independent-claim strategy. Keep the independent apparatus claim centered on the interaction between
 time-dependent state transitions, dual load paths, controlled translation and separately controlled rotation. Put
 specific hardware (cam/pawl, hydraulic cartridge, crush element, sensor/actuator type) into dependent claims unless
 the final prior-art search shows a specific mechanism to be the strongest novel feature.

 Alternative embodiments. The energy absorber may be hydraulic, pneumatic, frictional, viscoelastic, metallic crush,
 composite crush, or hybrid. The trigger may be passive inertial, electromechanical, pyrotechnic, software-controlled,
 or hybrid. The lock may use pawl/cam, wedge, ratchet, pin, dog clutch, or equivalent geometry. Such alternatives
 should be described to preserve claim breadth while the claims remain anchored to the time-dependent functional
 relationships.
 Abstract (draft). A vehicle seat system includes a seat structure coupled to a vehicle through at least two load paths
 and a time-dependent occupant-control mechanism. The mechanism transitions between normal, armed, controlled
 ride-down, rebound-control and secured states in response to vehicle or seat dynamics. During a crash event, the
 seat structure can translate through a characterized energy-management assembly while a distinct path limits
 seatback rotation. Pelvic geometry, restraint interfaces and distributed side structures cooperate to manage occupant
 trajectory and to distribute load into the vehicle structure. The system uses controlled inertial and contact forces
 rather than generating a local gravitational field. The architecture can be implemented in front or rear vehicle
 seating, including modular two-plus-one and three-seat rear-seat configurations.
